## Q3. What exactly does the queen order the huntsman to do and to bring back, and how does Snow White escape?

**Short answer:** In the final Grimm edition (1857) the queen orders the huntsman to take Snow White into the woods, kill her, and bring back her lungs and liver as proof. Snow White begs for her life, and the huntsman pities her because she is so beautiful and lets her run. He kills a young boar and brings its lungs and liver instead, which the cook boils with salt and the queen eats, thinking they are Snow White's. Snow White runs all day through the forest until evening, when she finds the dwarfs' little house. Choice made: the film follows the 1857 final edition (Ashliman's translation, checked against the German text). The 1812 first edition tells this scene the same way, so nothing here changes between editions, except that in 1812 the woman giving the order is Snow White's own mother (see Q2). Warning for the script: the well-known Margaret Hunt English translation (1884) says "heart" instead of "lungs and liver". The German text says lungs and liver, so the script should say lungs and liver.

**Worth showing:** the huntsman leading the small silhouette into the woods and raising his knife; the knife lowering as she pleads and he waves her away; a young boar darting past and the huntsman returning to the castle with the "proof"; the queen at the table eating it under the glowing mirror; the girl running alone over stones and through thorns while animal shapes leap past without harming her, as the light fades to evening and a small house appears.

## Gaps

- No folklore scholarship opened on why the organs are lungs and liver (some translations and adaptations use the heart). The script should simply follow the German text.
- Margaret Hunt's 1884 translation (Project Gutenberg #5314, https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5314/5314-h/5314-h.htm, opened) gives "heart" as the token. That is a translation difference, not an edition difference; the German 1857 text says "Lunge und Leber". Do not use "heart" in narration.
- UNVERIFIED: Snow White is seven years old when the mirror first names her the fairest (and so at roughly the time of the huntsman scene). Not checked on an opened page in this run; belongs to Q1.
- The 1812 German text was not opened; the 1812 details rest only on Ashliman's English translation (Q3-F3).
