## Q2. Who is the antagonist in the Grimm text: is she Snow White's mother or stepmother, and did this change between the Grimm editions?

- **Q2-F1** In the Grimms' first edition (1812), Snow White's jealous antagonist is her own mother, not a stepmother.
  - Source: [Ashliman, "Snow-White and other tales of type 709" (Little Snow-White, version of 1812)](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/type0709.html) · academic · undated · scope: Germany (1812 text)
  - Quote: "Snow-White's jealous antagonist is her own mother, not a stepmother."
  - Source 2: [Fine Books Magazine, "Grimm Brothers' First Edition of Fairy Tales in English: Not For the Faint of Heart" (Barbara Basbanes Richter)](https://www.finebooksmagazine.com/fine-books-news/grimm-brothers-first-edition-fairy-tales-english-not-faint-heart) · news · published 2014-11 · scope: Germany (1812 text)
  - Quote 2: "readers are confronted with evil biological mothers wishing their innocent children dead"
  - Flags: none [Source 2 talks about the first edition in general, reviewing Zipes's translation; it does not name Snow White in that sentence. Q2-F2 checks the claim directly against the 1812 German text.]
  - Check: VERIFIED (Source 1, verbatim in the "differences between the edition of 1812 and later versions" list: "Note that in the first edition, presumably the version closest to its oral sources, Snow-White's jealous antagonist is her own mother, not a stepmother." The same sentence is also in the grimm053.html notes. Source 2's quote is present but general, as flagged.)

- **Q2-F2** In the 1812 text, the queen who pricks her finger and wishes for a child is the one who gives birth to Snow White, and she is also the queen who later "became pale with envy" when the mirror names Snow White fairest.
  - Source: [Ashliman, Little Snow-White, version of 1812 (translation)](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/type0709.html) · academic · undated · scope: Germany (1812 text)
  - Quote: "Soon afterward she had a little daughter that was as white as snow" / "When the queen heard the mirror say this, she became pale with envy, and from that hour on, she hated Snow-White."
  - Source 2: [Wikisource, "Sneewittchen (Schneeweißchen)" (1812), Kinder- und Haus-Märchen Band 1, pp. 238–250, Realschulbuchhandlung, Berlin](https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/Sneewittchen_(Schneeweißchen)_(1812)) · data service (transcription of the primary text) · undated · scope: Germany (1812 text)
  - Quote 2: „Und bald darauf bekam sie ein Töchterlein" / „Wie die Königin den Spiegel so sprechen hörte, ward sie blaß vor Neid,"
  - Flags: none [Source 2 is the original German text, not a separate analysis. It confirms the wording, not a second interpretation.]
  - Check: VERIFIED (both sources. Both quotes are on both pages, and neither text has a queen dying or a king remarrying between the birth and the envy line; the 1812 German main text never mentions a king at all. WARN: the 1812 German appendix to vol. 1 notes a variant with a widowed king; that is outside the tale text and does not affect the claim.)

- **Q2-F3** The change came with the second edition of 1819: from then on, the Grimms say Snow White's mother died in childbirth and her father remarried.
  - Source: [Ashliman, "Little Snow-White" (Grimm 053), notes](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/grimm053.html) · academic · undated · scope: Germany (1819 onward)
  - Quote: Beginning with the 1819 edition, the Grimms state that Snow-White's mother died during childbirth and her father remarried. (paraphrase from WebFetch, not verbatim; check the note's exact wording)
  - Source 2: [Wikisource, "Sneewittchen" (1819), Kinder- und Haus-Märchen Band 1, 2nd ed., pp. 262–274, G. Reimer, Berlin](https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/Sneewittchen_(1819)) · data service (transcription of the primary text) · undated · scope: Germany (1819 text)
  - Quote 2: „Und wie das Kind geboren war, starb die Königin." / „Ueber ein Jahr nahm sich der König eine andere Gemahlin,"
  - Flags: none [WebFetch would not return the Ashliman note word for word. The German 1819 text confirms the change directly.]
  - Check: VERIFIED (both sources. The exact wording of Ashliman's note is "Beginning with the edition of 1819, the Grimms add the statement that Snow-White's mother died during childbirth, and that her father remarried." The Research quote is a close paraphrase of it. The Wikisource 1819 header confirms "2. Auflage", and both German lines are there word for word.)

- **Q2-F4** In the final 1857 edition, the queen dies as soon as Snow White is born, and a year later the king takes another wife: a beautiful but proud woman who cannot stand anyone being more beautiful than she is.
  - Source: [Ashliman, "Little Snow-White" (Grimm 053), translated from the final edition (Berlin, 1857), no. 53](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/grimm053.html) · academic · undated · scope: Germany (1857 text)
  - Quote: "And as soon as the child was born, the queen died." / "A year later the king took himself another wife."
  - Source 2: [Wikisource, "Sneewittchen" (1819)](https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/Sneewittchen_(1819)) · data service · undated · scope: Germany (1819 text)
  - Quote 2: „sie war eine schöne Frau, aber stolz auf ihre Schönheit," „und konnte nicht leiden, daß sie von jemand darin sollte übertroffen werden."
  - Flags: none [Source 2 is the 1819 text, which has the same wording. I did not open the 1857 German text. Ashliman's 1857 line for her character: "She was a beautiful woman, but she was proud and arrogant, and she could not stand it if anyone might surpass her in beauty."]
  - Check: VERIFIED (Source 1, which covers the 1857 text: both quoted sentences are there word for word, plus "She was a beautiful woman, but she was proud and arrogant, and she could not stand it if anyone might surpass her in beauty." Paraphrased words: "cannot stand anyone being more beautiful" for "could not stand it if anyone might surpass her in beauty". Source 2 is the 1819 text, so on its own it would not cover the 1857 claim.)

- **Q2-F5** The Grimms published seven editions of the tale, from 1812 to 1857. The 1857 seventh edition is the final one the brothers revised themselves (the "Ausgabe letzter Hand").
  - Source: [Wikisource, "Schneewittchen" (list of versions)](https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/Schneewittchen) · data service · undated · scope: Germany
  - Quote: "1812 (Erstausgabe)" … "1819 (2. Auflage)" … "1857 (7. Auflage bzw. Ausgabe letzter Hand)"
  - Source 2: [Ashliman, Grimm 053](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/grimm053.html) · academic · undated · scope: Germany
  - Quote 2: "final edition (Berlin, 1857), no. 53." (cut off)
  - Flags: none [Source 2 confirms only that 1857 is the final edition, not the count of seven. If the script says "seven", treat it as SINGLE SOURCE.]
  - Check: VERIFIED (Source 1 lists seven editions, 1812, 1819, 1837, 1840, 1843, 1850 and 1857, with 1857 labeled "7. Auflage bzw. Ausgabe letzter Hand". Paraphrased words: "the final one the brothers revised themselves" for "Ausgabe letzter Hand". Source 2 confirms only "final edition (Berlin, 1857)" and does not give the number seven. WARN: these seven are the editions of the large edition ("Große Ausgabe"). Say "seven editions of the full collection", not seven printings in total.)

- **Q2-F6** Ashliman notes that the 1812 first edition, where the villain is the mother, is presumably the version closest to the oral tales the Grimms collected.
  - Source: [Ashliman, Grimm 053, notes](https://sites.pitt.edu/~dash/grimm053.html) · academic · undated · scope: Germany
  - Quote: "In the first edition, presumably the version closest to its oral sources," (cut off)
  - Flags: SINGLE SOURCE [This is a scholar's judgment, not a fixed fact. Collecting methods are out of scope, so use it at most as a passing phrase.]
  - Check: VERIFIED (The full sentence on the page is "In the first edition, presumably the version closest to its oral sources, Snow-White's jealous antagonist is her own mother, not a stepmother." The Research quote is cut off before the villain part. Paraphrased words: "oral tales the Grimms collected" for "oral sources". WARN: keep "presumably"; this is Ashliman's hedge, not an established fact.)

- **Q2-F7** Folklorist Maria Tatar (Harvard) writes that the Grimms, wanting to protect the sanctity of motherhood, often turned biological mothers into stepmothers.
  - Source: [Maria Tatar, "Snow White and the Huntsman," Breezes from Wonderland (Harvard Blogs)](https://archive.blogs.harvard.edu/tatar/?p=1620) · academic (scholar's blog) · published 2012-06 · scope: Grimm tales generally
  - Quote: "The Grimms, in an effort to preserve the sanctity of motherhood, were forever turning biological mothers into stepmothers."
  - Flags: SINGLE SOURCE [This is an interpretation of why they did it. Tatar's post does not compare the Snow White editions directly. Wikipedia credits a similar view to Jack Zipes ("held motherhood sacred"), but I could not open Zipes's own text; see Gaps.]
  - Check: VERIFIED (The quote is on the page word for word, in a post dated June 8, 2012 on Maria Tatar's blog "Breezes from Wonderland", hosted on Harvard's blog domain. The post does not print an author byline, but the blog is hers and the post is written in the first person. Paraphrased words: "often turned" for "were forever turning", and "wanting to protect" for "in an effort to preserve". WARN: this is Tatar's view of the Grimms' motive across their tales in general, not a documented reason for the Snow White change. Present it as her interpretation, e.g. "as folklorist Maria Tatar puts it".)
