## Q4. For the best-selling electric cars of the latest model year, what are the battery size (kWh), rated range and fastest charging time (for example, 10–80%), and how much do cold and hot weather change range and charging speed?

**Short answer:** The three cars below hold batteries of about 81–85 kWh and are EPA-rated at roughly 320–357 miles. Their fastest charging times differ a lot: about 20 minutes for 10–80% in the Hyundai Ioniq 5, about 28 minutes in the Tesla Model Y, and about 77 miles added in 10 minutes in the Chevrolet Equinox EV. Cold weather does far more harm than heat. At 20°F, range falls by about 30% (real-world fleet data) to 39% (AAA lab test). At 90–95°F the loss is about 5–8.5%, and it rises to 17–18% only at 100°F. A cold battery also charges more slowly unless it is warmed first. Choices I made: "best-selling" means US sales in 2025, the latest full year (Kelley Blue Book data), because I found no full-year global model ranking on a page I could open. I picked the top seller from each of three carmakers: Tesla Model Y (#1), Chevrolet Equinox EV (#3, the best-selling non-Tesla) and Hyundai Ioniq 5 (#5). I skipped the Model 3 (#2), which uses the same maker's technology as the Model Y, and the Ford Mustang Mach-E (#4). I used model year 2026 because it is the latest year with full EPA ratings: fueleconomy.gov's 2027 listings so far show only the Model Y L for Tesla, and the 2027 Ioniq 5 keeps the 2026 range figures. Weather figures are US data.

- **Q4-F8** Cold weather cuts range sharply. In AAA's 2026 lab test, three EVs at 20°F lost 39% of their calculated driving range compared with 75°F. In real-world winter 2025/26 data from more than 30,000 US EVs, cars kept about 70% of their maximum range at 20°F and 78% at 32°F.
  - Source: [AAA Newsroom: AAA Study Reveals Temperature Impacts on EV and Hybrid Efficiencies and Costs](https://newsroom.aaa.com/2026/05/aaa-study-reveals-temperature-impacts-on-ev-and-hybrid-performance-efficiency-and-costs/) · industry · published 2026-05 · scope: US
  - Quote: "EVs demonstrate a 35.6% drop in MPGe and a 39.0% decrease in calculated driving range."
  - Source 2: [Recurrent: Best EV for Winter & Cold Weather Range](https://www.recurrentauto.com/research/winter-ev-range-loss) · industry · published 2025-11 · scope: US
  - Quote 2: "Even at 20F, cars retain an average of 70% of their maximum range."
  - Flags: none [Not a conflict: the methods differ. AAA used a lab dynamometer, only 3 EVs, the cabin heater set to 72°F and a 75°F baseline. Recurrent used real-world driving of 30,000+ vehicles compared with each model's maximum range, which implies about a 30% loss. Recurrent also reports a best model at 88% and a worst at 69% at 32°F, and finds that heat pumps add about 10% range at 32°F. In a separate road test of 14 EVs at -7 to -15°C, the Canadian Automobile Association measured losses from 14% to 39% ([KBB, 2025-02](https://www.kbb.com/car-news/cold-weather-testing-shows-ev-range-loss-of-14-percent-to-much-more)).]
- **Q4-F9** Hot weather costs much less range than cold. In AAA's 2026 lab test, EVs lost 8.5% of their range at 95°F compared with 75°F. In real-world data from about 30,000 US EVs, range fell about 5% at 90°F and 17–18% at 100°F (2025).
  - Source: [AAA Newsroom: AAA Study Reveals Temperature Impacts on EV and Hybrid Efficiencies and Costs](https://newsroom.aaa.com/2026/05/aaa-study-reveals-temperature-impacts-on-ev-and-hybrid-performance-efficiency-and-costs/) · industry · published 2026-05 · scope: US
  - Quote: "an 8.5% loss of driving range compared to moderate temperature (75°F)"
  - Source 2: [Recurrent: How Hot Summer Weather Affects EV Range](https://www.recurrentauto.com/research/what-a-c-does-to-your-range) · industry · published 2025-06 · scope: US
  - Quote 2: "Electric cars shed only 5% of range in hot weather at 90F (32C)."
  - Flags: none [Not a conflict: different temperatures and methods. Recurrent also writes "When temperatures reach 100F (37F), range impacts can be 17-18%", where the page's "37F" appears to be a typo for 37°C. NPR reports that AAA's 2019 test found a larger hot-weather loss of 17% with a different set of cars.]
- **Q4-F10** A cold battery charges more slowly, so warming it first (preconditioning) helps. In one fleet study, DC fast charging at 0°F took about 9 minutes longer on average than in warm weather, not counting warm-up time. Without preconditioning, the first 30–45 minutes of a session may add almost no charge (data mid-2022 to 2023).
  - Source: [NPR via WUNC: How well can EVs handle the heat and the cold? AAA put them to the test](https://www.wunc.org/2026-05-01/how-well-can-evs-handle-the-heat-and-the-cold-aaa-put-them-to-the-test) · news · published 2026-05 · scope: US
  - Quote: "if you're fast-charging, try to do it on a battery that's been warmed up; charging is slower on a cold battery."
  - Source 2: [Recurrent: Study: EV Charging Speeds in Cold Temperatures](https://www.recurrentauto.com/research/study-ev-charging-speeds-in-cold-temperatures) · industry · published 2024-01 · scope: US
  - Quote 2: "9 minutes longer on average, not including time to warm the battery if it is very cold."
  - Flags: SINGLE SOURCE for "9 minutes" and "30–45 minutes" [NPR/AAA and CAA (KBB: "EV chargers slow down in the cold, too.") confirm only that charging slows in the cold. Recurrent's numbers come from 200,000+ sessions, mostly Tesla Model 3 fast-charger visits since mid-2022, published January 2024, so the data predates the brief's 2024-onward rule. The script agent should decide whether to say the numbers or only the direction.]

## Gaps

- Tesla's and Chevrolet's own spec pages returned 403, so there is no carmaker-published 10–80% time for the Model Y or Equinox EV. The Model Y charging time is an EV Database estimate for the European version, and the Equinox EV has only a "miles in 10 minutes" figure, not a 10–80% time.
- The Model Y battery size (about 81–82 kWh) is not published by Tesla; it rests on KBB plus a European EV Database entry for a related version.
- No page I opened gave figures for how hot weather changes charging speed. Recurrent's heat article gives only tips (for example, keeping the battery between 50% and 80%).
- No 2024-or-later number for how much longer fast charging takes in the cold. Recurrent's 9-minute figure uses 2022–2023 data.
- Global best-seller: UNVERIFIED. The Tesla Model Y is widely reported as the world's best-selling electric car in 2025, but I did not open a page with full-year global model rankings, so the selection rests on US 2025 sales.
- The Tesla Model 3 (#2 in US 2025 sales) and Ford Mustang Mach-E (#4) were not researched (choice explained in the short answer).
- Calculation: EV Database's estimated real-world combined range for the European Model Y Premium AWD is 400 km in cold weather (-10°C, heater on) vs 550 km in mild weather (23°C). (550 − 400) / 550 = 150 / 550 ≈ 27% less in the cold. For the Ioniq 5 84 kWh RWD it is 380 km vs 520 km: (520 − 380) / 520 = 140 / 520 ≈ 27% less. These are EV Database estimates, not measurements.
- Calculation: Recurrent's "70% of maximum range" at 20°F means a loss of 100% − 70% = 30%, compared with AAA's 39% at the same temperature (different methods; see the Q4-F8 note).
