## Q5. What makes an electric car battery lose capacity over time, how much capacity do batteries keep after 5 and 10 years according to large real-world fleet datasets, and what capacity warranties do major automakers offer?

- **Q5-F1** Battery capacity does not fall evenly. It drops a little faster in the first year or two as the battery settles in, then declines slowly and steadily for many years, and drops sharply only near the end of its life.
  - Source: [Geotab: How long do electric car batteries last?](https://www.geotab.com/blog/ev-battery-health/) · industry · published 2026-04 · scope: Geotab customer fleets (regions not stated)
  - Quote: "Many EVs show a sharper drop in capacity in the first year or two before the rate levels out."
  - Source 2: [Recurrent: How long do EV batteries last?](https://www.recurrentauto.com/research/how-long-do-ev-batteries-last) · industry · published 2025-11 · scope: US
  - Quote 2: "After that, there is a long period of slow, linear aging, followed by a sharp decrease when the battery dies."
  - Flags: none [Recurrent also says "Almost all of the EVs on the road today are in their stable state."]
  - Check: VERIFIED (by the two sources together. Neither page covers the whole claim alone. Geotab gives the sharper early drop "in the first year or two" that then levels out, but says nothing about the end of life. Recurrent describes the "S-shaped curve": early loss "as the battery settles into its steady state", then "a long period of slow, linear aging", then "a sharp decrease when the battery dies". It gives no timeframe for the early phase. Paraphrased: "for many years" for "a long period"; "near the end of its life" for "when the battery dies".)

- **Q5-F2** Heat speeds up battery wear. In Geotab's 2025 fleet analysis, EVs in hot climates lost about 0.4 percentage points more capacity per year than EVs in mild climates. A US Department of Energy study suggests batteries may last 12–15 years in moderate climates but 8–12 years in severe climates.
  - Source: [Geotab: How long do electric car batteries last?](https://www.geotab.com/blog/ev-battery-health/) · industry · published 2026-04 · scope: Geotab customer fleets (regions not stated)
  - Quote: "heat increases chemical activity and stress inside the cell" … "vehicles operating in hot conditions degrading 0.4% faster per year than those in mild climates."
  - Source 2: [FuelEconomy.gov: Electric Vehicles: Learn More About the Technology](https://www.fueleconomy.gov/feg/evtech.shtml) · official · undated · scope: US
  - Quote 2: "a DOE study suggests these batteries may last 12 to 15 years in moderate climates and 8 to 12 years in severe climates"
  - Flags: SINGLE SOURCE [for the 0.4-point figure. Source 2 confirms that harsh climates shorten battery life but measures lifespan in years, not the yearly loss. The DOE study's date is not given.]
  - Check: VERIFIED (Geotab covers the first two sentences and fueleconomy.gov the third. Geotab's page reads "vehicles operating in hot climates on average degrade 0.4% faster per year than those in mild climates" and calls this "a moderate effect". The research quote's "hot conditions degrading" is not the exact page wording. Geotab defines "hot" as more than 35% of days above 25°C. The fueleconomy.gov sentence matches word for word. Paraphrased: "0.4 percentage points more capacity" for "0.4% faster". WARN: the page writes "0.4%", not "percentage points". Since the yearly losses on the page are 1.4–3.0%, reading it as points is natural, but the safe wording is "about 0.4% more capacity a year". WARN: fueleconomy.gov does not mention heat, does not define "severe climates", and does not name or date the DOE study. Do not present the DOE line as a finding about heat.)

- **Q5-F3** Frequent use of high-power DC fast charging roughly doubles the rate of capacity loss. In Geotab's 2025 analysis, heavy high-power fast-charging users lost about 3.0% a year, compared with about 1.5% a year for the low-power charging group.
  - Source: [Geotab: How long do electric car batteries last?](https://www.geotab.com/blog/ev-battery-health/) · industry · published 2026-04 · scope: Geotab customer fleets (regions not stated)
  - Quote: "degradation rates up to twice that of the low power charging group (3.0% vs 1.5% per year)"
  - Flags: SINGLE SOURCE [Geotab calls charging power the largest stressor it measured]
  - Check: VERIFIED (Geotab's Key Insights read "High-power DC fast charging (>100kW) is the single largest stressor, leading to degradation rates up to twice that of the low power charging group (3.0% vs 1.5% per year)." Paraphrased: "heavy high-power fast-charging users" for Geotab's High-Frequency High-Power group: DC fast charging on more than 12% of sessions, with over 40% of those sessions above 100 kW. WARN: the page's own table gives 1.5% to the "Low Frequency" group, meaning cars that used DC fast charging on under 12% of sessions, not to a low-power group. Frequent fast-chargers that mostly used lower power lost 2.2% a year. The script should say "compared with cars that rarely fast-charge", and "up to twice" rather than "always doubles".)

- **Q5-F4** Leaving a battery nearly full or nearly empty most of the time also speeds up wear. Vehicles that spent over 80% of their time below 20% or above 80% charge lost about 2.0% a year in Geotab's 2025 analysis, versus 1.4% for the low-exposure group.
  - Source: [Geotab: How long do electric car batteries last?](https://www.geotab.com/blog/ev-battery-health/) · industry · published 2026-04 · scope: Geotab customer fleets (regions not stated)
  - Quote: "extreme SOC levels (below 20% or above 80%)" … "averaging 2.0% per year" … "The degradation rates for the low (1.4%) and medium (1.5%) groups"
  - Flags: SINGLE SOURCE
  - Check: VERIFIED (Geotab defines extreme state of charge as "below 20% or above 80%" and high exposure as above 80% of total time at extreme SOC. The rates are 2.0% a year for high exposure, 1.4% for low exposure (under 50% of time) and 1.5% for medium. Paraphrased: "nearly full or nearly empty" for "extreme SOC levels". WARN: Geotab's comparison includes only vehicles with low DC fast-charging use ("To control for power, this chart only includes vehicles with low DCFC usage"). The page also says moderate exposure "does not significantly accelerate degradation", so keep "most of the time" and do not suggest that any time spent near full or empty does harm.)

- **Q5-F5** Across more than 22,700 fleet EVs covering 21 models, Geotab's 2025 analysis found that batteries lost on average 2.3% of their capacity per year. At that rate, Geotab projects the average battery will still hold about 81.6% of its original capacity after eight years.
  - Source: [Geotab: How long do electric car batteries last?](https://www.geotab.com/blog/ev-battery-health/) · industry · published 2026-04 · scope: Geotab customer fleets (regions not stated)
  - Quote: "The average annual electric vehicle degradation rate is 2.3%." … "the average battery is projected to have 81.6% of its original capacity"
  - Flags: SINGLE SOURCE [The 81.6% is a projection, not a measurement of 8-year-old cars. It differs from Generational's measured UK median of 85.04% for 8–12-year-olds (Q5-F7) because the two use different methods, regions and age groups, so this is not a conflict.]
  - Check: VERIFIED (Geotab: "Our 2025 analysis of over 22,700 electric vehicles, covering 21 different vehicle models"; "The average annual electric vehicle degradation rate is 2.3%."; "projected to have 81.6% of its original capacity (state of health or SOH) after eight years." Paraphrased: "fleet EVs" for the vehicles in Geotab's customer base. "At that rate" is not on the page, but 100 − 8 × 2.3 = 81.6 exactly. WARN: the page's FAQ calls the updated study the "2026 study", while the body says "Our 2025 analysis". The 81.6% is a projection, not a measurement.)

- **Q5-F6** About five years in: UK used EVs aged four to five years had a median battery health of 93.53% of original capacity, according to Generational's 2025 Battery Performance Index. The index covers more than 8,000 tested cars and vans from 36 manufacturers.
  - Source: [EV Fleet World: Average used EV battery health stands at 95.15%, real-world data shows](https://evfleetworld.co.uk/average-used-ev-battery-health-stands-at-95-15-real-world-data-shows/) · news (reporting Generational's industry study) · published 2026-02 · scope: UK
  - Quote: "Even within the four- to five-year cohort, median SoH remains strong at 93.53%."
  - Flags: SINGLE SOURCE [No other opened dataset reports capacity for this age band. The middle half of 4–5-year-olds sat between 91.64% and 96.49%.]
  - Check: VERIFIED (EV Fleet World: "Generational, a UK-based innovator in EV battery condition diagnostics, analysed more than 8,000 passenger cars and LCVs". It names "The 2025 Battery Performance Index", "the largest analysis of electric vehicle battery condition undertaken in the UK", which "draws on battery assessments conducted across 36 manufacturers". The page gives a median of 93.53%, with the 25th percentile at 91.64% and the 75th at 96.49%. Paraphrased: "vans" for "LCVs", "tested" for "battery assessments", "of original capacity" for "SoH" (the page measures health "compared to new"), and "About five years in" for the four- to five-year cohort.)

- **Q5-F8** Aviloo's study of more than 500,000 independent battery tests on 20 popular EV models (2022–2026) found that cars which had covered 150,000 km still had 87–94% battery health. Cars of the same model could differ by up to 13.5%.
  - Source: [The Driven: New study of more than 500,000 used EVs shows battery health is doing much better than most people think](https://thedriven.io/2026/09/11/new-study-of-more-than-500000-used-evs-shows-battery-health-is-doing-much-better-than-most-people-think/) · news (reporting Aviloo's industry study) · published 2026-09 · scope: global (tests run worldwide)
  - Quote: "cars covering 150,000 km had between 87-94% battery health remaining"
  - Flags: SINGLE SOURCE [Results are by distance driven, not by age. Aviloo measured battery health with its own test kit, not the car's dashboard reading.]
  - Check: VERIFIED (The Driven names Aviloo, which "runs battery testing kits globally", and describes "over 500,000 independent battery health tests globally", with "500,000 tests run between 2022 and 2026 across 20 popular EV models". The quote matches the page. On spread within a model, the page says "Battery health also varies within the same model" and the capacity remaining "can differ by up to 13.5% between the best and worst examples". Paraphrased: "Aviloo's study" for the article's "a new study by an Austrian battery health testing company"; "more than" for "over". WARN: the article does not say whether 87–94% is the spread across models or across individual cars. The 13.5% is the gap between the best and worst cars of one model, not a loss rate.)

- **Q5-F9** In Europe, Volkswagen and Hyundai both use the same benchmark: the high-voltage battery is covered for 8 years or 160,000 km (100,000 miles for Volkswagen UK) to at least 70% of its original capacity. These terms are from their warranty pages as checked in October 2026.
  - Source: [Volkswagen UK: Warranties](https://www.volkswagen.co.uk/en/owners-and-services/my-car/warranties.html) · company · undated (page footer © 2026) · scope: UK
  - Quote: "eight year or 100,000 miles/160,000km high voltage battery warranty" … "as long as the reduction in capacity does not fall below 70%"
  - Source 2: [Hyundai Europe: Warranty](https://www.hyundai.com/eu/en/driving-hyundai/owning-a-hyundai/warranty.html) · company · undated · scope: Europe
  - Quote 2: "are covered for 8 years, or up to 160,000 km" … "return the battery State of Health (capacity) to at least 70% of its original state."
  - Flags: none [Each source is the automaker's own warranty, and both give 8 years / 160,000 km and 70%. Hyundai words it as warranty repairs restoring at least 70%.]
  - Check: VERIFIED (Volkswagen UK covers the Volkswagen half and Hyundai Europe the Hyundai half. Volkswagen UK: "an eight year or 100,000 miles/160,000km high voltage battery warranty (whichever is soonest)" for the ID.3, ID.4, ID.5 and ID.7. Capacity loss is not a defect "as long as the reduction in capacity does not fall below 70% of the vehicle's battery capacity at first registration". Hyundai Europe: "covered for 8 years, or up to 160,000 km", with repairs to "return the battery State of Health (capacity) to at least 70% of its original state". Both pages were opened on 2026-10-07. Paraphrased: "original capacity" for Volkswagen's "capacity at first registration". WARN: the Volkswagen terms come only from the UK page, so say "Volkswagen in the UK" rather than implying all of Europe. Both terms end at whichever limit comes first. The 70% is the point at which a warranty repair applies, not the capacity owners should expect.)

- **Q5-F10** In the US, Kia covers EV batteries for capacity loss below 70% of the original for 10 years or 100,000 miles, whichever comes first. That is longer than the common 8-year term.
  - Source: [Kia America: Warranty](https://www.kia.com/us/en/warranty) · company · undated (page footer © 2026, comparison data "as of December 2024") · scope: US
  - Quote: "covering capacity loss below 70% of the original capacity for 10 years or 100,000 miles, whichever comes first."
  - Flags: SINGLE SOURCE [This is Kia's official warranty page. Hyundai's matching US 10-year term could not be confirmed on an opened page; see Gaps.]
  - Check: VERIFIED (The Kia America quote matches the page. The page adds "Repairs or replacements restore the battery to at least 70% capacity." For "longer than the common 8-year term", the page's table ("Comparison of the warranty periods of leading car brands") lists Toyota, Honda, Nissan, Chevrolet and Ford at "8-year / 100,000 miles" of EV battery coverage. Paraphrased: "common" for the five competitors in Kia's table. WARN: the comparison is Kia's own, "based on comparison of warranty specifications on manufacturer websites as of December 2024", so competitor terms may have changed since.)
