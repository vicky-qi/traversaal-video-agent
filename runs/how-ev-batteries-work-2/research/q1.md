## Q1. Inside a single electric car battery cell, what are the main parts, and what physically moves between them when the battery is charging and when it is powering the car?

**Short answer:** A lithium-ion cell has two electrodes, each coated onto a thin metal foil: a negative one usually made of graphite and a positive one made of lithium metal oxide particles. Between them sit a porous separator that keeps them from touching and a liquid or gel electrolyte. When the battery powers something, lithium ions travel inside the cell from the graphite side to the metal oxide side. Electrons cannot pass the separator, so they take the outside route through the circuit, and that flow of electrons is the electricity. Charging pushes the ions back to the graphite side. The ions slot into the layered electrodes and shuttle back and forth instead of wearing the electrodes away, which is why the cell can be recharged. Choice made: this question is about how a cell works, so I used general lithium-ion explainers, which cover the cell family used in passenger electric cars. They are not specific to one car or chemistry; Q3 covers the different chemistries.

- **Q1-F1** A single lithium-ion cell has five main parts: a positive electrode (cathode), a negative electrode (anode), a porous separator between them, a liquid or gel electrolyte, and thin metal current collectors (general explanation, not tied to a date).

  - Source 2: [Battery University, "BU-204: How do Lithium Batteries Work?"](https://batteryuniversity.com/article/bu-204-how-do-lithium-batteries-work) · industry · updated 2022-02 · scope: global
  - Quote 2: "Lithium-ion uses a cathode (positive electrode), an anode (negative electrode) and electrolyte as conductor."
  - Flags: none [This Source 2 quote names three of the parts. The same page also names the separator and the current collectors, see Q1-F3 and Q1-F4.]

- **Q1-F2** The negative electrode (anode) is usually made of graphite, a form of carbon. The positive electrode (cathode) is a layer of lithium metal oxide particles, for example lithium cobalt oxide or lithium iron phosphate (general explanation).
  - Source: [Pacific Northwest National Laboratory, "Lithium-ion Battery (LFP and NMC)"](https://www.pnnl.gov/projects/esgc-cost-performance/lithium-ion-battery) · official · undated · scope: global
  - Quote: "charge and discharge reactions from a lithiated metal oxide cathode and a graphite anode"
  - Source 2: [Ionworks, "Batteries 101: How Do Batteries Work?"](https://www.ionworks.com/blog/batteries-101-how-do-batteries-work) · company · published 2025-01 · scope: global
  - Quote 2: "This is a porous layer typically made of lithium-metal oxide particles (e.g., lithium cobalt oxide or lithium iron phosphate)."
  - Flags: none [Ionworks also says the anode is "typically made of graphite", and Battery University says "carbon/graphite". Which cathode recipes cars use, and in what shares, belongs to Q3.]

- **Q1-F3** Each electrode is coated onto a thin metal foil called a current collector: aluminum on the positive side and copper on the negative side (general explanation).
  - Source: [Ionworks, "Batteries 101: How Do Batteries Work?"](https://www.ionworks.com/blog/batteries-101-how-do-batteries-work) · company · published 2025-01 · scope: global
  - Quote: "Thin metal foils (typically aluminum for the positive electrode and copper for the negative one)"
  - Source 2: [Battery University, "BU-204: How do Lithium Batteries Work?"](https://batteryuniversity.com/article/bu-204-how-do-lithium-batteries-work) · industry · updated 2022-02 · scope: global
  - Quote 2: "a negative electrode (anode) made from carbon/graphite coated on a copper current collector,"
  - Flags: none [The same Battery University sentence says the positive electrode "is coated onto an aluminum current collector". Ionworks says the foils connect the electrodes to the outside circuit.]

- **Q1-F4** The electrolyte is a liquid or gel, usually a lithium salt dissolved in an organic solvent. It soaks the electrodes and separator and lets lithium ions move between the two sides (general explanation).
  - Source: [Ionworks, "Batteries 101: How Do Batteries Work?"](https://www.ionworks.com/blog/batteries-101-how-do-batteries-work) · company · published 2025-01 · scope: global
  - Quote: "A liquid or gel that fills the pores within the electrodes and separator, facilitating the movement of lithium ions."
  - Source 2: [Battery University, "BU-204: How do Lithium Batteries Work?"](https://batteryuniversity.com/article/bu-204-how-do-lithium-batteries-work) · industry · updated 2022-02 · scope: global
  - Quote 2: "a separator and electrolyte made of lithium salt in an organic solvent."
  - Flags: none [Ionworks also says the electrolyte "usually consists of a lithium salt dissolved in an organic solvent."]

- **Q1-F5** The separator is a thin, porous sheet between the electrodes. It stops them from touching, which would cause a short circuit, but lithium ions are small enough to pass through it (general explanation).
  - Source: [Ionworks, "Batteries 101: How Do Batteries Work?"](https://www.ionworks.com/blog/batteries-101-how-do-batteries-work) · company · published 2025-01 · scope: global
  - Quote: "A thin, porous membrane that keeps the positive and negative electrodes apart to prevent short circuits, while allowing ions to pass through."
  - Source 2: [University of Washington Clean Energy Institute, "Lithium-Ion Battery"](https://www.cei.washington.edu/research/energy-storage/lithium-ion-battery/) · academic · undated · scope: global
  - Quote 2: "The lithium ions are small enough to be able to move through a micro-permeable separator between the anode and cathode."
  - Flags: none

- **Q1-F6** When the battery is powering something (discharging), lithium ions leave the negative graphite electrode and travel inside the cell, through the electrolyte and separator, to the positive electrode. At the same time, electrons leave the negative electrode by the outside route, through the external circuit, which they power, and re-enter the cell at the positive electrode (general explanation).
  - Source: [Ionworks, "Batteries 101: How Do Batteries Work?"](https://www.ionworks.com/blog/batteries-101-how-do-batteries-work) · company · published 2025-01 · scope: global
  - Quote: "Simultaneously, electrons are released from the negative electrode, travel through the external circuit (powering the device), and re-enter the battery at the positive electrode."
  - Source 2: [Battery University, "BU-204: How do Lithium Batteries Work?"](https://batteryuniversity.com/article/bu-204-how-do-lithium-batteries-work) · industry · updated 2022-02 · scope: global
  - Quote 2: "During discharge, the ions flow from the anode to the cathode through the electrolyte and separator;"
  - Flags: none [Ionworks describes the ion path too: lithium ions "leave the negative electrode (deintercalate) and travel through the electrolyte and separator". UW Clean Energy Institute agrees that "The lithium ions move from the anode and pass through the electrolyte until they reach the cathode." No opened page names a car's motor as the "external circuit"; see Gaps.]

- **Q1-F7** When the battery is charging, the charger applies an outside voltage that reverses the process: lithium ions move back from the positive electrode to the negative graphite electrode, returning the cell to its high-energy (charged) state (general explanation).
  - Source: [Ionworks, "Batteries 101: How Do Batteries Work?"](https://www.ionworks.com/blog/batteries-101-how-do-batteries-work) · company · published 2025-01 · scope: global
  - Quote: "Lithium ions move back to the negative electrode, restoring the battery to its high-energy state."
  - Source 2: [Battery University, "BU-204: How do Lithium Batteries Work?"](https://batteryuniversity.com/article/bu-204-how-do-lithium-batteries-work) · industry · updated 2022-02 · scope: global
  - Quote 2: "charge reverses the direction and the ions flow from the cathode to the anode."
  - Flags: none [Ionworks' previous sentence reads "By applying an external voltage to the battery, the process is reversed."]

- **Q1-F8** The lithium ions are stored by slipping into spaces in the layered structure of the electrodes, a process called "intercalation". The battery works by ions shuttling back and forth between the two sides, not by chemical reactions that break the electrodes down, and this is why it can be recharged over and over (general explanation).
  - Source: [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, "The Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2019"](https://kva.se/en/news/nobelpriset-i-kemi-2019-2) · academic · published 2019-10 · scope: global
  - Quote: "they are not based upon chemical reactions that break down the electrodes, but upon lithium ions flowing back and forth between the anode and cathode"
  - Source 2: [Ionworks, "Batteries 101: How Do Batteries Work?"](https://www.ionworks.com/blog/batteries-101-how-do-batteries-work) · company · published 2025-01 · scope: global
  - Quote 2: "Lithium ions are stored (or "intercalated") within the layers of the negative electrode."
  - Flags: none [The Academy also describes an early electrode material that "has spaces that can house – intercalate – lithium ions." It says the first commercial battery "could be charged hundreds of times before its performance deteriorated". That is a single-source historical figure about 1990s batteries, so do not say it as a figure for today's cars; real-world aging belongs to Q5.]

- **Q1-F9** A single lithium-ion cell supplies only about 3.6 volts (a typical "nominal" figure for lithium-ion cells in general, not tied to a date).
