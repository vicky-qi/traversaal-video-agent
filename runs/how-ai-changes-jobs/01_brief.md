# Video Brief: How AI Is Changing Jobs

**Assumptions:** The prompt "AI and jobs" names a topic but no audience, angle or scope. I assumed (1) a general business audience with no technical or economics background; (2) "AI" means the current wave of AI software tools that write, summarize, analyze and code, not factory robots or self-driving vehicles; (3) a balanced, evidence-based view of both risks and opportunities, rather than a prediction of mass job loss or a promotional pitch; (4) a mainly U.S. focus for data, with international comparisons where good data exists.

- **Original prompt:** "AI and jobs"
- **Audience:** Professionals, managers and students who use or hear about AI tools at work and wonder what it means for their careers and teams; they know what AI chatbots are but have no technical or economics background.
- **Goal:** After watching, the viewer understands how AI is affecting work so far, which kinds of tasks and jobs are most and least affected, and what practical steps workers and managers can take to adapt.
- **Angle:** Instead of asking "Will AI take my job?", the video asks which tasks inside a job AI can do, and uses the evidence so far to show who is gaining, who is at risk, and how to adapt.
- **Tone:** Curious, clear and balanced: neither alarmist nor hype, practical at the end
- **Length:** about 7 minutes, about 1,000 words of narration

## Key questions for research

1. When earlier technologies automated work (for example ATMs for bank tellers, or spreadsheets for bookkeepers), how did the number of people employed in those occupations change in the years that followed?
2. According to recent research by government agencies, international organizations and academic economists, which occupations and which kinds of tasks are most and least exposed to AI, and how do they define "exposed"?
3. What share of U.S. companies and U.S. workers report using AI tools at work, and how has that share changed each year from 2023 to today?
4. In controlled experiments in real workplaces (for example customer support, professional writing or software coding), how much did AI tools change the speed or quality of work, and did less-experienced workers gain more or less than experienced ones?
5. Since 2023, how have employment and job postings changed in the occupations rated most exposed to AI compared with less-exposed occupations, especially for entry-level and recent-graduate roles?
6. Which job titles and skills have grown fastest in job postings because of AI since 2023, and do postings that ask for AI skills offer higher pay than similar postings that do not?
7. Which large companies have publicly linked layoffs or hiring slowdowns to AI, which have announced retraining or redeployment programs instead, and what do employer surveys say about plans to reskill workers?

## Out of scope

- Factory robots, warehouse automation and self-driving vehicles (beyond a passing mention)
- How AI models work technically
- Long-range predictions about superhuman AI or a "world without work"
- Policy debates such as universal basic income or taxing AI, beyond a brief mention
- AI used to screen job applicants and hiring bias (a separate topic)
- Detailed career advice for individual professions or country-by-country comparisons

## Notes for the researcher

- Best source types: government labor statistics (employment, wages and job openings by occupation); reports from international economic and labor organizations; academic papers and working papers by economists, especially field experiments in real workplaces; data published by large job-posting and professional-networking platforms; company annual reports, earnings calls and official press releases; major news outlets for layoff and hiring announcements.
- Figures on AI adoption, employment, job postings, pay and company announcements must be from 2023 onward; use the most recent year available and note the date of each figure. Historical automation figures (question 1) may be older but must come from a reliable historical dataset or study.
- Clearly separate what has already been measured from forecasts about the future; for any forecast, record who made it and when.
- When a news story says a company cut jobs "because of AI," check the company's own statement and record its exact wording.
- Prefer U.S. data; add international comparisons only where the data is equally reliable.
