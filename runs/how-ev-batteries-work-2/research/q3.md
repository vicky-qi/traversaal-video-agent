## Q3. Which cell chemistries are used in passenger electric cars sold in the latest full year, what share of new electric cars uses each, and how do they compare on energy stored per kilogram, cost per kWh and fire safety?

**Short answer:** Two families of battery recipe dominate. LFP (lithium iron phosphate) cells made up more than half of EV batteries worldwide in 2025. Nickel-based cells (NMC, nickel manganese cobalt, and NCA, nickel cobalt aluminium) made up most of the rest. Where they are used depends heavily on region. LFP made up about four-fifths of installations in China, but only just over 10% in the EU, and its share fell in the US. Nickel-based cells store about 30% more energy per kilogram. LFP packs cost roughly 35–40% less per kWh, and LFP cells tend to tolerate more heat before thermal runaway, although their off-gas is more flammable.

Choices I made:
- The latest full year is 2025.
- No source gives shares by number of cars sold. Shares are shares of battery capacity (GWh) deployed in EVs. That figure includes vans, buses and trucks; IEA leaves out only two- and three-wheelers.
- The scope is global, with China, EU and US splits.
- IEA counts LMFP inside LFP. Sodium-ion was still marginal in 2025 and is left to Q7.

- **Q3-F10** LFP is not risk-free. A review of 60 studies found that NMC cells release more gas when they fail. LFP's gas, however, is more toxic in absolute terms and catches fire more easily: its lower flammable limit is 6.2%, against 7.9% for NMC.
  - Source: [University of Sheffield, "New research paves the way for safer batteries with reduced fire and toxicity risks"](https://sheffield.ac.uk/cmbe/news/new-research-paves-way-safer-batteries-reduced-fire-and-toxicity-risks) · academic · published 2024-09 · scope: global (literature review)
  - Quote: "NMC batteries release more gas than LFP." · "LFP batteries present a greater flammability hazard." · "the LFL levels are for LFP 6.2% and NMC 7.9%."
  - Flags: SINGLE SOURCE [The study is a meta-analysis published in the Journal of Energy Storage by researchers at Sheffield, Imperial College London and St Andrews, according to [pv magazine USA](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2024/04/10/how-safe-are-lfp-batteries/), which reports the same study and so is not independent. Flammability limits were measured in an inert atmosphere. The script should present safety as a trade-off and not say "LFP can't catch fire".]

## Gaps

- **Global share has only one source.** I found no second independent source for the global LFP share (F1). The Rho Motion data reported by EV Magazine and InsideEVs (two attempts: insideevs.com, insideevs.de) could not be opened (403/502).
- UNVERIFIED (search snippets only): Rho Motion found that LFP kWh deployment in passenger and light-duty EVs passed nickel-based chemistries for the first time in 2025, with LFP up 43% and nickel-based up 8% in January–November.
- UNVERIFIED (search snippets only): Benchmark Mineral Intelligence put LFP at about 61% of global battery demand in 2025. That figure probably covers all uses, including stationary storage.
- **Shares are by battery capacity, not by car.** No source gives the share of new electric cars, counted as cars, by chemistry. All the shares above are battery capacity (GWh) and include vans, buses and trucks. The IEA says LFP powers "two-thirds of all electric car sales" in emerging economies, which is car-specific, but I left it out because the exact country group needs checking on the page.
- **Europe and US numbers are vague.** The only figures are "more than 10%" for the EU and "almost halved" for the US. No precise percentages were found.
- **Energy per kg has only one source** (IEA). The PowerUp page quotes older CEA-Liten measured ranges: LFP "90 to 130 Wh/kg" and NMC "190 to 260Wh/kg". Those cover older, typical cells, not the IEA's "latest generation" maximums. They measure different things, so they are not a conflict and are not used as Source 2.
- **Cost by chemistry for car batteries alone is not public.** BNEF's chemistry split for EV batteries alone is available only to clients, so F6 mixes car and stationary-storage packs.
- **Fire safety lacks numbers from a government or peer-reviewed page.** Blocked pages: ScienceDirect (Evro et al. 2024), DOAJ (2025 *Batteries* paper), Electric & Hybrid Vehicle Technology International (403, and its digital edition was empty), and an FAA/UL Fire Safety Research Institute presentation (503). The SINTEF paper abstracts I opened give no LFP-vs-NMC figures.
- UNVERIFIED: unlike NMC, LFP's cathode does not release oxygen when overheated. This is a common explanation for LFP's better heat tolerance, seen only in Wikipedia's summary of peer-reviewed papers.
- UNVERIFIED (search snippet; page 403): one heating test found an NMC prismatic cell went unstable at about 160°C and an LFP cell at about 230°C, with peak cell-face temperatures of about 800°C (NMC) and 620°C (LFP).
- UNVERIFIED (search snippet; page 403): a 2025 *Batteries* journal paper reported critical thermal-runaway temperatures of 346°C for LFP, 290°C for NCM523 and 281°C for NCM622.
- **Sodium-ion is out of scope here.** Sodium-ion cells (IEA: up to 175 Wh/kg) and LMFP were negligible or folded into LFP in 2025. They are left to Q7.
- Calculation (my own arithmetic): BNEF price gap = (128 − 81) ÷ 128 = 47 ÷ 128 ≈ 0.367. That makes LFP packs about 37% cheaper per kWh than NMC in 2025, against the IEA's "more than 40%". The relative difference is (40 − 36.7) ÷ 40 ≈ 8%, which is under the 10% conflict threshold.
- Calculation (my own arithmetic): energy per kg, 265 ÷ 205 ≈ 1.29. The latest NMC cells store about 29% more energy per kg than the latest LFP cells. Put the other way, 205 ÷ 265 ≈ 0.77, so LFP stores about 23% less.
- Calculation (my own arithmetic): China non-LFP share = 100% − 81.2% = 18.8%, consistent with the reported 18.7% ternary share (rounding).
