## Q1. Inside a single electric car battery cell, what are the main parts, and what physically moves between them when the battery is charging and when it is powering the car?

**Short answer:** A lithium-ion cell has two electrodes, each coated onto a thin metal foil: a negative one usually made of graphite and a positive one made of lithium metal oxide particles. Between them sit a porous separator that keeps them from touching and a liquid or gel electrolyte. When the battery powers something, lithium ions travel inside the cell from the graphite side to the metal oxide side. Electrons cannot pass the separator, so they take the outside route through the circuit, and that flow of electrons is the electricity. Charging pushes the ions back to the graphite side. The ions slot into the layered electrodes and shuttle back and forth instead of wearing the electrodes away, which is why the cell can be recharged. Choice made: this question is about how a cell works, so I used general lithium-ion explainers, which cover the cell family used in passenger electric cars. They are not specific to one car or chemistry; Q3 covers the different chemistries.

## Q2. How are individual cells grouped and wired into modules and a full pack in a typical passenger electric car, and what do the battery management system and the cooling system monitor and control?

**Short answer:** A car battery is built in layers: cells are first joined side by side (in parallel) into small groups to add capacity, those groups are chained end to end (in series) to add voltage and bundled into modules, and the modules make up a pack that runs at hundreds of volts. Some newer designs skip the module layer and put cells straight into the pack. The battery management system measures voltage, current and temperature, estimates how full and how healthy the battery is, evens out the cells, and cuts the battery off if anything goes outside safe limits; the cooling system, run by the same electronics, keeps the cells in a safe temperature range and close to each other in temperature. Choice made: the brief leaves "typical car" open, so the worked example is the Chevrolet Bolt EV (2022 model, 65 kWh), because its maker publishes the full cell-to-pack layout; the brief allows how-it-works basics from any year.

  - Flags: none

- **Q2-F10** Temperature affects a battery's life, performance and safety (heat wears cells out faster, cold cuts available power and energy), so the cooling system keeps cells in a target temperature range and evens out temperature differences between cells; the battery electronics run the coolant pumps and fans based on cell temperatures.

## Q3. Which cell chemistries are used in passenger electric cars sold in the latest full year, what share of new electric cars uses each, and how do they compare on energy stored per kilogram, cost per kWh and fire safety?

**Short answer:** Two families of battery recipe dominate. LFP (lithium iron phosphate) cells made up more than half of EV batteries worldwide in 2025. Nickel-based cells (NMC, nickel manganese cobalt, and NCA, nickel cobalt aluminium) made up most of the rest. Where they are used depends heavily on region. LFP made up about four-fifths of installations in China, but only just over 10% in the EU, and its share fell in the US. Nickel-based cells store about 30% more energy per kilogram. LFP packs cost roughly 35–40% less per kWh, and LFP cells tend to tolerate more heat before thermal runaway, although their off-gas is more flammable.

## Q5. What makes an electric car battery lose capacity over time, how much capacity do batteries keep after 5 and 10 years according to large real-world fleet datasets, and what capacity warranties do major automakers offer?

**Short answer:** Batteries wear out slowly with time and use. Their capacity drops a little faster in the first year or two, then declines slowly and steadily. Heat, frequent high-power fast charging and spending most of the time nearly full or nearly empty all speed up the loss. Large real-world datasets show average losses of about 2% a year. Geotab measured 2.3% a year across 22,700+ fleet EVs. UK used cars aged 4–5 years kept a median 93.5% of capacity, and 8–12-year-old cars kept about 85%. Automakers commonly guarantee at least 70% of original capacity for 8 years / 100,000 miles (160,000 km), and Kia offers 10 years / 100,000 miles in the US.

Choices made: no opened dataset reports exactly "10 years", so I used the 8–12-year age group, the closest available. ## Q7. Which next-generation battery types have major automakers or battery makers announced for mass production, with what target dates, and what gains in range, charging time or cost do they claim?
