## Q1. In the Grimms' published text of "Little Snow White" (Sneewittchen), what is the plot in order, scene by scene, and which edition is the standard source (first edition versus the final revised edition)?

**Short answer:** The Grimms published seven editions of their tale collection between 1812 and 1857; the seventh (1857) is the "Ausgabe letzter Hand", the last edition the brothers prepared themselves, and it is the text most readers and translations use. **Choice made: the film follows the final 1857 edition**, in D. L. Ashliman's English translation (University of Pittsburgh), checked against the German 1857 text on German Wikisource. In order, the 1857 plot runs: a queen pricks her finger and wishes for a child, then dies at the birth; the king remarries a vain queen with a magic mirror; when Snow-White is seven the mirror names her fairer; the queen orders a huntsman to kill her and bring back her lungs and liver; he spares her and she reaches the seven dwarfs' cottage; the queen comes three times in disguise (laces, poisoned comb, poisoned apple); the dwarfs lay her in a glass coffin; a prince takes the coffin, a servant stumbles, the apple piece comes loose; at the wedding the stepmother is made to dance in red-hot iron shoes until she dies. Two points differ in the 1812 first edition (flagged in Q1-F10): the villain is Snow-White's own mother, and the apple is knocked loose when a servant strikes her in the back.

**Worth showing:** three drops of blood falling onto snow beside a black window frame; the mirror answering each morning, then changing its answer when the girl turns seven; a small figure running deeper into the forest until a cottage appears; the queen returning three times in three disguises with three objects (laces, comb, half-red apple); a transparent glass coffin on a mountain; a coffin-bearer stumbling and the apple piece jolting loose; iron shoes heating over coals.

## Q2. Who is the antagonist in the Grimm text: is she Snow White's mother or stepmother, and did this change between the Grimm editions?

**Short answer:** It changed. In the first edition (1812) the jealous queen is Snow White's own mother: the same queen who wishes for a child white as snow later turns pale with envy at her daughter. From the second edition (1819) on, the Grimms added that the mother died in childbirth and the king remarried a year later, so the villain became a stepmother. That version stayed through the final edition of 1857. Choice made: the film should follow the **final 1857 edition**, where the queen is a stepmother. This is the standard text, and the main one Ashliman translates. The 1812 "own mother" detail is best used as a single line of contrast ("in the very first printing, she was her mother").

**Worth showing:** One paper-cut queen silhouette at the window, who wishes for the child and then, in the 1812 version, turns into the jealous queen at the mirror. Then the 1819 change: the first queen's silhouette fades out after the birth, and a second, prouder queen slides in on a new paper layer. A row of seven book spines (1812 to 1857) could show that the change came in the second printing and lasted.

## Q3. What exactly does the queen order the huntsman to do and to bring back, and how does Snow White escape?

**Short answer:** In the final Grimm edition (1857) the queen orders the huntsman to take Snow White into the woods, kill her, and bring back her lungs and liver as proof. Snow White begs for her life, and the huntsman pities her because she is so beautiful and lets her run. He kills a young boar and brings its lungs and liver instead, which the cook boils with salt and the queen eats, thinking they are Snow White's. Snow White runs all day through the forest until evening, when she finds the dwarfs' little house. Choice made: the film follows the 1857 final edition (Ashliman's translation, checked against the German text). The 1812 first edition tells this scene the same way, so nothing here changes between editions, except that in 1812 the woman giving the order is Snow White's own mother (see Q2). Warning for the script: the well-known Margaret Hunt English translation (1884) says "heart" instead of "lungs and liver". The German text says lungs and liver, so the script should say lungs and liver.

**Worth showing:** the huntsman leading the small silhouette into the woods and raising his knife; the knife lowering as she pleads and he waves her away; a young boar darting past and the huntsman returning to the castle with the "proof"; the queen at the table eating it under the glowing mirror; the girl running alone over stones and through thorns while animal shapes leap past without harming her, as the light fades to evening and a small house appears.

## Q4. How many times does the queen try to kill Snow White, what disguise and object does she use each time, and how is Snow White saved after each attempt?

**Short answer:** After the huntsman fails her (see Q3), the queen comes to the dwarfs' cottage in disguise three times: first as an old peddler woman selling bodice laces, which she pulls so tight Snow White stops breathing; then as a different old woman with a poisoned comb; and finally as a peasant (country) woman with an apple poisoned only on its red half. The dwarfs save her the first two times, by cutting the laces and by pulling out the comb, but after the apple nothing they do helps, and she is revived only later when the piece of apple is jolted out of her throat while the coffin is carried. The film follows the final 1857 edition (Ashliman's translation, checked against Margaret Hunt's); the three attempts are the same in the 1812 first edition, but the antagonist there is Snow White's own mother, and the apple comes loose in a different way (a servant strikes her instead of stumbling).

**Worth showing:** The three visits as a repeating rhythm: a cloaked silhouette at the cottage door, each time in a new disguise; laces pulled tight around a paper figure, then snipped apart; a comb sliding into her hair, then pulled out; an apple cut in two, white half and red half, with the queen eating the white; the dwarfs gathered around a still figure, failing the third time; the coffin bearers stumbling and the apple piece dropping free.

## Q5. How does Snow White wake in the Grimm text (what causes the poisoned piece to come loose), and what happens to the queen at the wedding?

**Short answer:** In the final Grimm edition (1857, the 7th edition, which is the standard text), a prince talks the dwarfs into giving him the glass coffin. As his servants carry it away on their shoulders, they stumble over a shrub, the jolt shakes the bit of poisoned apple out of Snow White's throat, and she sits up alive. The stepmother is invited to the wedding, sees Snow White there, and is made to put on iron shoes heated red-hot in the coals and dance until she falls dead. **Choice made:** this file follows the 1857 final edition (D. L. Ashliman's English translation, checked against the German text on Wikisource), because that is the version almost all modern readers and translations use. The 1812 first edition differs on both points, flagged in Q5-F9 and Q5-F10: an angry servant hits her on the back to wake her, and the woman at the wedding is her own mother.

**Worth showing:** the glass coffin lifted onto servants' shoulders, tilting as they stumble over a shrub, and the apple piece flying loose; Snow White lifting the lid and sitting up; the gold mirror glowing one last time to call the "young queen" a thousand times fairer; the queen frozen at the wedding door; iron shoes glowing in the coals, lifted out with tongs, then a frantic dance that ends in a fall. ## Q6. Which of these Grimm details are changed or left out in the best-known modern film adaptation, according to reputable folklore scholarship or major publications?

**Short answer:** The best-known modern adaptation is taken to be Walt Disney's animated *Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs* (premiered December 1937). The Grimm side is the final edition (1857) in D. L. Ashliman's translation, so that it lines up with Q1–Q5. Compared with that text, the film cuts the queen's first two attempts (the laces and the comb) and keeps only the poisoned apple. It wakes Snow White with "love's first kiss" instead of a servant stumbling while carrying the coffin. It replaces the red-hot iron shoes at the wedding with the queen falling from rocks in a storm. It gives the unnamed Grimm dwarfs names and personalities, and it makes Snow White a teenager rather than "hardly more than a child". Both versions make the villain a stepmother: the Grimms' 1812 first edition made her Snow White's own mother, but they changed that from 1819, so this is not a Disney change.

**Worth showing:** A split page with the Grimm half and the familiar half. On the Grimm half, the queen knocks three times (laces, comb, apple); on the familiar half she knocks once, with the apple. Coffin bearers stumble and the apple piece flies loose, set against a single kiss. Glowing red-hot iron shoes, set against a cloaked figure falling from rocks in a storm. Seven identical blank dwarf silhouettes that each take on a different shape and a name.

## Gaps

- The claim that 1857 is "the standard source" rests on its status as the Grimms' own final edition (Staatsbibliothek, Wikisource header) and on Gidwitz comparing the 1857 text with the familiar film. I did not open a scholarly page that states outright that most English translations follow 1857. Britannica's "Grimm's Fairy Tales" page returned 403.
- UNVERIFIED: Margaret Hunt's 1884 translation was made from a late (1857-based) edition; her rendering of "heart" instead of "lungs and liver" suggests a softened or different source wording. Not confirmed on any page I opened.
- UNVERIFIED: the Wikipedia statement (from search results only) that Jack Zipes links the stepmother change to the Grimms holding "motherhood sacred". Not opened; relevant to Q2, not Q1.
- The Ashliman type 709 page (1812 text) returned summarized rather than verbatim text for the servant striking Snow-White; the fact-checker should confirm that line on the page.
- The English Wikisource page for Hunt's translation would not return verbatim quotes, so it is not cited as a source; it was used only to spot the "heart" translation difference.
- The 1812 text appears to have no king and no remarriage. WebFetch's summary of the Wikisource 1812 page said "No king appears" in the main text, but I have no verbatim quote showing this. Check the 1812 page before the script says "there is no king/father in the first version".
- UNVERIFIED: Jack Zipes's explanation that the Grimms made the change because they "held motherhood sacred", and that the revision was likely Wilhelm Grimm's work. I saw this only second-hand in a search summary of Wikipedia's "Evil Queen" article, and did not open Zipes's own text.
- UNVERIFIED: Whether the 1815 volume or any edition after 1819 changed the antagonist again. The 1819 and 1857 texts both have a stepmother, but I did not open the 1837 to 1850 texts. Ashliman's "Beginning with the 1819 edition" wording suggests the change held throughout.
- WebFetch would not give the exact wording of Ashliman's note on the 1819 change, so Q2-F3's first quote is a paraphrase. The fact-checker should copy the exact sentence from the live page.
- No folklore scholarship opened on why the organs are lungs and liver (some translations and adaptations use the heart). The script should simply follow the German text.
- Margaret Hunt's 1884 translation (Project Gutenberg #5314, https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5314/5314-h/5314-h.htm, opened) gives "heart" as the token. That is a translation difference, not an edition difference; the German 1857 text says "Lunge und Leber". Do not use "heart" in narration.
- UNVERIFIED: Snow White is seven years old when the mirror first names her the fairest (and so at roughly the time of the huntsman scene). Not checked on an opened page in this run; belongs to Q1.
- The 1812 German text was not opened; the 1812 details rest only on Ashliman's English translation (Q3-F3).
- The count of "three" attempts is read from the text (three disguised visits); neither translation page states the number outright. If the huntsman order (Q3) is counted, the queen tries four times; the script should say "three times in disguise" to avoid ambiguity.
- The German original (Sneewittchen, 1857) was not opened in this run; both English translations agree on all plot points above.
- Ashliman's 1857 and 1812 translations are by the same translator, so the edition comparison relies on one translator's notes; the 1812 changes are stated on his type 709 page.
- UNVERIFIED: Some Hunt quotes came from WebFetch summaries of a very long page (offset 420000); exact wording should be checked on the live page, especially "took the shape of another old woman."
- Ashliman's 1857 page gives the place of publication as "Berlin, 1857"; German Wikisource lists the 1857 edition as Dieterich, Göttingen. I did not settle this. The script does not need the city; the fact-checker may want to look into it.

Search results pointed to Jack Zipes's translation of the first edition (*The Original Folk and Fairy Tales of the Brothers Grimm*, 2014), but I did not open it.
